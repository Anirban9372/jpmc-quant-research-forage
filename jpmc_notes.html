Domain Knowledge Dictionary

Commodities & Natural Gas

MMBtu Million British Thermal Units
The standard unit of energy for natural gas pricing. One MMBtu ≈ the energy in 1,000 cubic feet of natural gas. Prices in our dataset are in $/MMBtu — dollars per unit of energy delivered.
Henry Hub The US natural gas pricing benchmark
A natural gas pipeline interchange in Louisiana that serves as the pricing point for NYMEX natural gas futures. "Henry Hub price" = the reference price for US natural gas. Our dataset represents delivery prices linked to this benchmark.
Commodity Storage Contract The instrument we're pricing in Tasks 1 & 2
A legal agreement between a storage facility owner and a market participant. The client pays to store a physical commodity (gas, oil, grain) for a set period. The economic logic: buy cheap (summer), store, sell expensive (winter). The contract spells out:
  • Injection dates: when the commodity is purchased and pumped in
  • Withdrawal dates: when it's pumped out and sold
  • Injection/withdrawal rate: how fast it can be pumped (volume per day)
  • Max storage: the physical capacity of the facility
  • Storage cost: fee paid to the facility owner per unit of time/volume
Working Gas vs Base Gas What storage tracks
Base gas (cushion gas): the permanent inventory kept in the facility to maintain pressure — it never leaves.
Working gas: the tradeable volume on top of base gas. When traders talk about "gas in storage," they mean working gas. This is what follows the seasonal build-in-summer, draw-in-winter pattern.
Contango vs Backwardation Futures curve shapes
Contango: future prices higher than spot (today's) price. Gas in summer is cheaper than gas in winter — the futures curve is in contango. This is when storage contracts make money (buy spot, sell futures).
Backwardation: future prices lower than spot. Storage contracts lose value here. High storage utilization → contango. Low utilization → often backwardation.

Credit Risk Terms

PD — Probability of Default What Task 3 models
The probability that a borrower will stop making required payments within a defined time horizon (typically 1 year). Output of our logistic regression. A number between 0 and 1. PD = 0.85 means an 85% chance of default.
LGD — Loss Given Default What fraction you lose if they default
If a borrower defaults, the bank doesn't lose everything — they can recover some value (collateral, legal proceedings). LGD = 1 − Recovery Rate. In our simulation: Recovery Rate = 10%, so LGD = 90%. Real mortgages have LGD ~40–60%; unsecured personal loans can be 60–90%.
EAD — Exposure at Default How much is owed when they default
The total amount outstanding at the time of default. For a simple term loan, EAD = remaining loan balance. In our model: EAD = loan_amt_outstanding (the current outstanding balance).
Expected Loss (EL) The Basel formula — what banks must provision for
EL = PD × LGD × EAD
Banks (under Basel III regulation) must hold capital reserves equal to their expected losses. Our expected_loss() function computes exactly this. A portfolio of 10,000 loans all at PD=0.185, LGD=0.9, average EAD=$20,000 would have expected loss = 0.185 × 0.9 × 20,000 × 10,000 = $33.3 million.
FICO Score Standardized US creditworthiness score
Created by the Fair Isaac Corporation. Range: 300 (worst) to 850 (best). Used in 90% of US mortgage decisions. Factors: payment history (35%), amounts owed (30%), length of credit history (15%), new credit (10%), credit mix (10%). In our data, FICO strongly predicts default: scores below 521 had a 66% default rate vs 4.6% above 697.

Math / ML Terms

Quantization Converting continuous values to discrete buckets
The process of mapping a continuous or high-cardinality variable (like FICO 300–850) into a small number of discrete categories. Used when a model requires categorical input. The challenge: where to draw the boundaries so information loss is minimized. We optimize boundary placement using log-likelihood (Task 4).
Log-Likelihood (Bernoulli) The objective function in Task 4
For a binary event (default / no default), each observation is a Bernoulli trial. The likelihood of observing k defaults out of n borrowers, given default probability p, is:
L = p^k × (1-p)^(n-k)
Taking the log (for numerical stability and to convert products to sums):
ℓ = k·log(p) + (n-k)·log(1-p)
Maximizing this over all buckets finds bucket boundaries where each bucket's internal default rate is as homogeneous (consistent) as possible. If a bucket has p=0 or p=1 (perfectly pure), log(0) is undefined — we return 0 as a convention (no information gain, no penalty).
Dynamic Programming (DP) The algorithm in Task 4
A technique for solving optimization problems by breaking them into overlapping subproblems and storing (memoizing) results to avoid recomputation. Key requirements:
  • Optimal substructure: the optimal solution to the full problem contains optimal solutions to subproblems
  • Overlapping subproblems: the same subproblems appear many times
In Task 4: "the best way to place j buckets over FICO scores 0 to i" reuses "the best way to place j-1 buckets over 0 to m" for every possible m. Without DP: O(N^k) complexity. With DP: O(N² × k), where N=374 unique FICO values, k=5 buckets.
ROC-AUC Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve
A model evaluation metric for binary classifiers. The ROC curve plots True Positive Rate (TPR = TP/(TP+FN)) against False Positive Rate (FPR = FP/(FP+TN)) at every possible classification threshold. AUC = the area under this curve. AUC = 0.5 means random guessing. AUC = 1.0 means perfect classification. Better than accuracy for imbalanced datasets (18.5% defaults in ours) because it's insensitive to class imbalance.
Sinusoidal Regression The model in Task 1
Fitting a sine wave plus a linear trend to time series data. Used when data has a known periodic structure (seasons, daily cycles). The key mathematical trick: rewrite C·sin(ωt + φ) using the angle addition identity as C₁·sin(ωt) + C₂·cos(ωt), making it a linear problem in the unknowns C₁ and C₂ (no need to guess the phase φ directly).

Task 1 — Natural Gas Price Analysis

📋 Business Context

Who asked for it: Alex, VP on the Commodity Trading Desk.

The problem: External market data feeds only provide one natural gas price per month. A storage contract might inject on March 15th and withdraw on November 22nd — you need a price on those exact dates, not just "March" and "November."

What you need to deliver: A function estimate_price(date_str) that takes any date as a string and returns an estimated price in $/MMBtu. Must work for historical dates (already in dataset) and future dates up to one year beyond the last data point.

Data given: Nat_Gas.csv — 49 monthly price observations, Oct 31 2020 to Sep 30 2024.

Key constraint: Market holidays and weekends need not be accounted for. Interest rates are zero.

Why this matters for Task 2: The price_contract() function in Task 2 calls estimate_price() at every injection and withdrawal date. Without Task 1, Task 2 cannot run.

Goal: Take 49 monthly natural gas price observations (Oct 2020 – Sep 2024) and build a model that can return an estimated price for any date — historical or future.

The Math First

Natural gas has a known annual seasonality (winter demand spike) riding on a long-term price trend. Our model captures both:

P(t) = A + B·t + C₁·sin(ωt) + C₂·cos(ωt)

where t is time in months from the first data point, and ω = 2π/12 (one full cycle per 12 months).

Why sin and cos together instead of just sin?
We want to fit C·sin(ωt + φ) but φ (the phase) makes this nonlinear. Using the angle addition formula:
C·sin(ωt + φ) = C·cos(φ)·sin(ωt) + C·sin(φ)·cos(ωt) = C₁·sin(ωt) + C₂·cos(ωt)
Now C₁ and C₂ are just linear coefficients — solvable by ordinary least squares or scipy's curve_fit. The original amplitude and phase are recovered as: C = √(C₁² + C₂²), φ = arctan2(C₂, C₁).

Our fitted parameters: A=10.1349 (base price), B=0.0456 (rises $0.046/month), C₁=0.6901, C₂=-0.0379 (amplitude ≈ $0.69 seasonal swing).

curve_fit uses the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm (a hybrid of gradient descent and Gauss-Newton) to find parameter values that minimize the sum of squared residuals between model output and actual data. It returns optimal params and the covariance matrix of those params (tells you how uncertain each estimate is).

Cell 4: Model Definition and Fitting

def seasonal_model(t, A, B, C1, C2):
    omega = 2 * np.pi / 12          # one full cycle per 12 months
    return A + B*t + C1*np.sin(omega*t) + C2*np.cos(omega*t)

params, covariance = curve_fit(seasonal_model, t_data, p_data)
A, B, C1, C2 = params
omega = 2 * np.pi / 12
Angular frequency ω. One full sine wave completes in 2π radians. We want that to happen over 12 time units (months). So ω = 2π/12 ≈ 0.524 rad/month. At t=12: ω×12 = 2π → one full cycle. Correct.
curve_fit(seasonal_model, t_data, p_data)
Passes our function and data to Levenberg-Marquardt. It tries different values of A, B, C1, C2, evaluates seasonal_model(t, A, B, C1, C2) for each, compares to actual prices, and adjusts params to minimize Σ(predicted - actual)². Returns best params and covariance matrix.

Cell 6: The estimate_price() Function

reference_date = df['Dates'].iloc[0]  # Oct 31, 2020 → t=0

def estimate_price(date_str):
    date = pd.to_datetime(date_str)           # parse string → Timestamp
    t = (date - reference_date).days / 30.44  # convert to fractional months
    price = seasonal_model(t, A, B, C1, C2)   # evaluate model
    return round(price, 4)
(date - reference_date).days / 30.44
Subtracting two Timestamps gives a Timedelta. .days converts to integer days. Dividing by 30.44 (average days/month) converts to fractional months — keeping the same units as our t variable from training. This is how we extrapolate: a date in 2025 gives t=60+, and the model just evaluates there.

Key difference: Two-step approach

def bilinear_regression(y, x1, x2):
    slope1 = np.sum(y*x1) / np.sum(x1**2)   # projection onto sin basis
    slope2 = np.sum(y*x2) / np.sum(x2**2)   # projection onto cos basis
    return slope1, slope2
slope = np.sum((x-xbar)*(y-ybar)) / np.sum((x-xbar)**2)
This IS the OLS (Ordinary Least Squares) formula for slope, derived by minimizing Σ(yᵢ - (A + Bxᵢ))². The numerator is Σ(xᵢ-x̄)(yᵢ-ȳ) = sample covariance × n. The denominator is Σ(xᵢ-x̄)² = sample variance × n. Their ratio is the correlation times (σy/σx). Same result as scipy's linregress.
slope1 = np.sum(y*x1) / np.sum(x1**2)
Bilinear regression without intercept. This is vector projection: slope1 = (y · x1) / (x1 · x1). If x1 and x2 were orthogonal (they're not quite, but sin and cos are orthogonal over a full period), this would be exact. In practice it's a good approximation for this data.
time * 2*np.pi / 365
JPMC uses days as their time unit (not months). So the period needs to be 365 days, not 12. ω = 2π/365. This is more precise than our 30.44 approximation but requires day-level time indexing.

Task 2 — Commodity Storage Contract Pricing

📋 Business Context

Who asked for it: Alex, VP on the Commodity Trading Desk (same person as Task 1, next step).

The problem: The client believes winter 2023–24 will be colder than expected. They want to buy natural gas now (summer, lower price) and sell it in winter (higher price). The desk needs to quote a fair contract value before they can trade.

The economic logic: Contract Value = what you sell gas for − what you paid for it − all costs (storage fees, injection costs, withdrawal costs). If positive, the contract is profitable for the client; if negative, they wouldn't enter into it.

What you need to deliver: A function price_contract(...) with these inputs:

  • Injection dates (list of date strings — when to buy and store)
  • Withdrawal dates (list of date strings — when to sell)
  • Injection rate (MMBtu/day max pump-in speed)
  • Withdrawal rate (MMBtu/day max pump-out speed)
  • Max storage volume (MMBtu capacity of facility)
  • Storage cost (fee per MMBtu per day, or per month)
  • Injection/withdrawal cost (fee per MMBtu handled)

Key constraint: No transport delay. Interest rates zero. Cannot withdraw more than currently stored.

Worked example from Alex's brief: Buy 1M MMBtu at $2, store 4 months at $100K/month, sell at $3 → Gross profit $1M − $400K storage − $10K injection − $10K withdrawal − $100K transport each way = $580K net value.

Goal: Given injection dates, withdrawal dates, and cost parameters, compute the net value of a natural gas storage contract.

The Math First

A contract's value is simply: what you earn minus everything you spend.

V = Revenue from sales − Cost of gas purchases − Storage fees − Injection costs − Withdrawal costs

More precisely:

Revenue = Σ P_sell(t_out) × V_out

Purchase cost = Σ P_buy(t_in) × V_in

Storage cost = storage_rate × days_stored × volume_stored

Injection cost = injection_rate × total_volume_injected

Withdrawal cost = withdrawal_rate × total_volume_withdrawn

V = Revenue − Purchase − Storage − Injection − Withdrawal
Key constraint: you can never withdraw more than what's currently stored. The function must track running volume and cap withdrawals at the available quantity.

Our Code — Line by Line

def price_contract(injection_dates, withdrawal_dates,
                   injection_rate, withdrawal_rate,
                   max_storage, storage_cost_per_day,
                   injection_cost_per_mmbtu, withdrawal_cost_per_mmbtu):

    total_value = 0      # accumulates net value (starts at 0, goes negative then positive)
    current_volume = 0   # tracks how much gas is currently in storage

    # --- INJECTIONS ---
    for date_str in injection_dates:
        buy_price = estimate_price(date_str)   # calls our Task 1 model
        volume = min(injection_rate, max_storage - current_volume)  # can't overfill
        if volume <= 0:
            print(f"Warning: Storage full on {date_str}, skipping.")
            continue
        current_volume += volume
        total_value -= buy_price * volume               # we SPEND money buying gas
        total_value -= injection_cost_per_mmbtu * volume  # we SPEND on injection

    # --- STORAGE COST ---
    first_in = pd.to_datetime(injection_dates[0])
    last_out = pd.to_datetime(withdrawal_dates[-1])
    days_stored = (last_out - first_in).days
    total_value -= storage_cost_per_day * days_stored * current_volume  # volume-weighted

    # --- WITHDRAWALS ---
    for date_str in withdrawal_dates:
        sell_price = estimate_price(date_str)
        volume = min(withdrawal_rate, current_volume)  # can't withdraw more than stored
        if volume <= 0:
            print(f"Warning: Storage empty on {date_str}, skipping.")
            continue
        current_volume -= volume
        total_value += sell_price * volume                  # we EARN money selling gas
        total_value -= withdrawal_cost_per_mmbtu * volume  # we SPEND on withdrawal

    return round(total_value, 2)
volume = min(injection_rate, max_storage - current_volume)
Physical constraint: the storage facility has a finite capacity. You can only inject as much as fits in the remaining space. injection_rate is the maximum you could pump in a single date; (max_storage - current_volume) is the actual remaining space. We take the smaller of the two.
total_value -= storage_cost_per_day * days_stored * current_volume
Storage cost model: per MMBtu per day. A real-world example: holding 2,000,000 MMBtu for 180 days at $0.001/MMBtu/day costs $0.001 × 180 × 2,000,000 = $360,000. Note: this is computed AFTER injections, so current_volume reflects all injected gas. This assumes we hold the same volume for the entire period — a simplification (real models track volume changes over time).
total_value += sell_price * volume
Revenue: selling gas at the withdrawal date price. sell_price comes from estimate_price() — our Task 1 model. If winter prices are higher than summer injection prices, this is the source of profit in the contract.

JPMC Example Code — Line by Line

def price_contract(in_dates, in_prices, out_dates, out_prices,
                   rate, storage_cost_rate, total_vol, injection_withdrawal_cost_rate):
    volume = 0
    buy_cost = 0
    cash_in = 0

    all_dates = sorted(set(in_dates + out_dates))  # merge and sort ALL dates
    for start_date in all_dates:
        if start_date in in_dates:
            if volume <= total_vol - rate:   # enough space?
                volume += rate
                buy_cost += rate * in_prices[in_dates.index(start_date)]  # purchase cost
                buy_cost += rate * injection_withdrawal_cost_rate           # injection cost
        elif start_date in out_dates:
            if volume >= rate:               # enough gas?
                volume -= rate
                cash_in += rate * out_prices[out_dates.index(start_date)]  # revenue
                cash_in -= rate * injection_withdrawal_cost_rate             # withdrawal cost

    store_cost = math.ceil((max(out_dates) - min(in_dates)).days // 30) * storage_cost_rate
    return cash_in - store_cost - buy_cost
all_dates = sorted(set(in_dates + out_dates))
Critical design choice: JPMC merges injection and withdrawal dates into a single sorted timeline. This handles INTERLEAVED schedules: you could inject Jan 1, withdraw Jan 27 (before the month ends), inject Feb 1 — our approach (all injections first) would break on this. Their loop handles any ordering correctly.
store_cost = math.ceil((max(out_dates) - min(in_dates)).days // 30) * storage_cost_rate
JPMC's storage model: a FIXED MONTHLY FEE (like rent), not per-volume. The cost is computed as the number of months the storage is in use, multiplied by a flat rate. math.ceil rounds up — a 45-day contract pays for 2 months. This is a completely different cost structure from ours: theirs is independent of volume, ours scales with volume. Both reflect real contract types.
in_prices[in_dates.index(start_date)]
JPMC's function takes pre-supplied price lists (in_prices, out_prices) as parameters. It does NOT call estimate_price() internally. The caller is responsible for providing prices. Our approach calls estimate_price() inside — tighter coupling but cleaner API for automated quoting.

Task 3 — Credit Risk / Probability of Default

📋 Business Context

Who asked for it: Charlie, Associate on the Retail Banking Risk Team (completely different team from Tasks 1 & 2).

The problem: The retail banking arm has been seeing higher-than-expected default rates on personal loans. Regulation (Basel III) requires banks to hold capital reserves equal to their expected losses. To set aside the right amount of capital, you need a model that predicts how likely each borrower is to default.

Data given: Task_3_and_4_Loan_Data.csv — 10,000 borrowers with features: credit_lines_outstanding, loan_amt_outstanding, total_debt_outstanding, income, years_employed, fico_score, and binary target: default (0 or 1). 18.5% default rate.

What you need to deliver: A function expected_loss(...) that takes borrower characteristics and returns:

  • PD = Probability of Default (from your logistic regression model)
  • EL = PD × LGD × EAD, where LGD = 1 − 0.10 = 90%, EAD = loan_amt_outstanding

Recovery rate assumption: 10% — if a borrower defaults, the bank recovers 10 cents on every dollar owed (through collateral, legal collection, etc.), and loses the remaining 90 cents.

Why this matters: If the bank's loan book has 10,000 loans averaging $20,000 each at an average PD of 18.5%, the total expected loss = 0.185 × 0.90 × 20,000 × 10,000 = $33.3 million that must be held in reserve.

Goal: Build a model that takes borrower characteristics and returns the probability that borrower will default, then compute expected loss.

The Math First

We use Logistic Regression — the standard model for binary probability estimation.

P(default=1 | x) = σ(β₀ + β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ + ... + βₙxₙ)

where σ is the sigmoid function:

σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e⁻ᶻ)

The sigmoid squashes any real number into (0,1) — making it a valid probability. The model learns coefficients β by maximizing log-likelihood over the training data:

ℓ(β) = Σ [yᵢ·log(σ(xᵢβ)) + (1-yᵢ)·log(1-σ(xᵢβ))]

This is equivalent to minimizing binary cross-entropy loss.

The expected_loss() Function

def expected_loss(credit_lines_outstanding, loan_amt_outstanding,
                  total_debt_outstanding, income,
                  years_employed, fico_score,
                  recovery_rate=0.10):

    features = pd.DataFrame([[credit_lines_outstanding, loan_amt_outstanding,
                               total_debt_outstanding, income,
                               years_employed, fico_score]], columns=X.columns)

    features_scaled = scaler.transform(features)  # MUST use same scaler as training
    pd_prob = model.predict_proba(features_scaled)[0][1]  # PD

    lgd = 1 - recovery_rate   # 0.90 (lose 90% of loan if they default)
    ead = loan_amt_outstanding # amount at risk

    el = pd_prob * lgd * ead   # EL = PD × LGD × EAD

    return round(el, 2)

Task 4 — FICO Bucketing with Dynamic Programming

📋 Business Context

Who asked for it: Charlie, same associate from Task 3, but now working on the mortgage book (different loan product from Task 3's personal loans).

The problem: Charlie is building a new ML model that requires categorical (discrete) inputs. FICO scores are integers from 300–850 — 551 distinct values. Feeding raw integers would create too many categories and make the model unstable. She needs them mapped to a small number of risk buckets (e.g., 5).

The naive approach (wrong): Equal-width buckets: 300–410, 411–520, 521–630, 631–740, 741–850. Problem: these boundaries are arbitrary. They might lump together borrowers with very different default rates, or split borrowers who are actually similar.

The right approach: Find boundaries that maximize statistical separation between buckets — each bucket should have a distinct, internally consistent default rate. This is an optimization problem.

What you need to deliver:

  • An algorithm optimal_buckets(n) that takes a number of buckets and returns the optimal boundary FICO values
  • A function fico_to_rating(fico_score) that maps any FICO score to a rating 1 (best) to n (worst)
  • The algorithm must generalize — work for any n, any future dataset

Optimization objective: Maximize log-likelihood ℒ = Σᵢ [kᵢ·log(pᵢ) + (nᵢ−kᵢ)·log(1−pᵢ)] — the Bernoulli log-likelihood of each bucket's observed defaults given the bucket's default rate.

Why DP: Brute-forcing all C(374, 4) ≈ 760M boundary combinations for 5 buckets takes hundreds of seconds. DP reduces it to ~700K operations.

Goal: Map FICO scores (300–850) into n optimal buckets, where "optimal" means maximizing the log-likelihood of the bucket assignment — finding boundaries where default rates differ most strongly across buckets.

The Math First

The Objective Function

Maximize: ℒ = Σᵢ [kᵢ·log(pᵢ) + (nᵢ-kᵢ)·log(1-pᵢ)]

where for each bucket i: nᵢ = total borrowers, kᵢ = defaulters, pᵢ = kᵢ/nᵢ = default rate.

Why DP? The Brute Force Problem

With 374 unique FICO values and 5 buckets, we need to choose 4 boundary positions. That's C(374, 4) ≈ 760 million combinations. At 1 million evaluations per second, that takes 760 seconds. For 10 buckets: C(374, 9) ≈ 10¹⁷ combinations. Completely intractable.

The DP Recurrence

Optimal Substructure: If we know the optimal way to place j-1 buckets over FICO indices 0 to m, then the optimal j-bucket solution over 0 to i is: (optimal j-1 buckets over 0 to m) + (one bucket over m+1 to i), for the best possible m.
dp[i][j] = max over all m of: dp[m][j-1] + bucket_ll(m+1, i)

Base case: dp[i][1] = bucket_ll(0, i) — one bucket covering everything from 0 to i.

Complexity: O(N² × k) where N=374 and k=5. That's 374² × 5 ≈ 700,000 operations. Fast.

Prefix Sums — the O(1) bucket cost trick

Without prefix sums, computing nᵢ and kᵢ for any bucket requires scanning all points in that bucket — O(N) per bucket evaluation. With prefix sums:

prefix_n[i] = n₀ + n₁ + ... + nᵢ₋₁
n(l..r) = prefix_n[r+1] - prefix_n[l]
k(l..r) = prefix_k[r+1] - prefix_k[l]

Each bucket stat is now computed in O(1) — a single subtraction.

Our Code — Line by Line

Data Grouping

fico_data = (df.groupby('fico_score')['default']
               .agg(n='count', k='sum')   # n=total borrowers, k=defaulters
               .reset_index()
               .sort_values('fico_score')
               .reset_index(drop=True))
.agg(n='count', k='sum')
For each unique FICO score, count total borrowers (n) and sum defaults (k). The 'default' column is 0 or 1, so sum = total defaults. This reduces 10,000 rows to 374 unique FICO value groups — the unit of work for our DP.

Prefix Sums

prefix_n = np.zeros(n_scores + 1, dtype=int)  # one extra element for the "before 0" sentinel
prefix_k = np.zeros(n_scores + 1, dtype=int)

for i in range(n_scores):
    prefix_n[i+1] = prefix_n[i] + fico_data['n'].iloc[i]
    prefix_k[i+1] = prefix_k[i] + fico_data['k'].iloc[i]
prefix_n = np.zeros(n_scores + 1, ...)
Size is n_scores + 1 (not n_scores) because prefix_n[0] = 0 acts as a sentinel. This makes the range formula n(l..r) = prefix_n[r+1] - prefix_n[l] clean: for l=0, prefix_n[0]=0, so n(0..r) = prefix_n[r+1] - 0 = prefix_n[r+1]. No special case needed for the first element.

bucket_ll() — Single Bucket Log-Likelihood

def bucket_ll(l, r):
    n = prefix_n[r+1] - prefix_n[l]   # total borrowers in bucket
    k = prefix_k[r+1] - prefix_k[l]   # defaulters in bucket
    if n == 0 or k == 0 or k == n:    # edge cases: log(0) undefined
        return 0.0
    p = k / n
    return k * np.log(p) + (n - k) * np.log(1 - p)
if k == 0 or k == n: return 0.0
If k=0: p=0, log(0) = -∞. If k=n: p=1, log(0) = -∞. But these are perfectly pure buckets (all default or all non-default) — they're actually desirable! We return 0 instead of -∞ because: (1) log(0) crashes numerically, (2) a pure bucket contributes no uncertainty — treating it as 0 information gain is a reasonable convention that prevents the optimizer from penalizing good splits.

optimal_buckets() — The DP

def optimal_buckets(num_buckets):
    N = n_scores
    dp    = np.full((N, num_buckets + 1), -np.inf)  # -inf = "not yet computed"
    split = np.zeros((N, num_buckets + 1), dtype=int)  # stores backtrack pointers

    # Base case: 1 bucket covering 0..i
    for i in range(N):
        dp[i][1] = bucket_ll(0, i)

    # Fill for j=2,3,...,num_buckets
    for j in range(2, num_buckets + 1):
        for i in range(j-1, N):        # need at least j points for j buckets
            for m in range(j-2, i):    # last bucket = m+1..i, rest = 0..m in j-1 buckets
                val = dp[m][j-1] + bucket_ll(m+1, i)
                if val > dp[i][j]:
                    dp[i][j]    = val
                    split[i][j] = m+1  # remember where the last bucket starts

    # Backtrack to find actual boundaries
    boundaries = []
    i = N - 1
    j = num_buckets
    while j > 1:
        start = split[i][j]
        boundaries.append(fico_data['fico_score'].iloc[start])
        i = start - 1
        j -= 1
    boundaries.reverse()
    return boundaries
dp = np.full((N, num_buckets+1), -np.inf)
Initialize all DP cells to -infinity. This ensures: any valid placement (finite log-likelihood) will always beat the initial value. If a cell is still -inf after the loop, it means that configuration is impossible (e.g., fitting 5 buckets into 3 data points).
for m in range(j-2, i): val = dp[m][j-1] + bucket_ll(m+1, i)
Core DP recurrence. For each possible "last bucket start" m+1: the last bucket covers m+1 to i (cost = bucket_ll(m+1, i)), and everything before is optimally handled by j-1 buckets (cost = dp[m][j-1]). We try all valid m and keep the best. j-2 is the minimum: need at least j-1 data points for j-1 previous buckets.
split[i][j] = m+1
The split table stores "where did the last bucket start?" for each (i, j) combination. This is the backtracking pointer. After DP fills the table, we trace backward through split to recover the actual boundary positions.
boundaries.reverse()
Backtracking finds boundaries from right-to-left (last bucket first). We reverse to get them in ascending FICO order (left-to-right, low-to-high FICO).

Critical Warnings to Remember

Look-ahead bias: In Task 1, we used all 49 data points to fit the model, including data from 2023–2024 to extrapolate back to 2021 prices. In a live trading system, the model at any date can only use data available BEFORE that date. Always fit on a rolling window for backtesting.
Synthetic data trap: ROC-AUC = 1.0 in Task 3 looks good but is a warning sign. Real credit data has AUC 0.70–0.85. Never trust a "perfect" model without investigating for data leakage or synthetic data artifacts.
Fit on train only: StandardScaler, any imputer, any feature selector — always fit on training data, transform test data using training statistics. Fitting on test data leaks information.
Storage cost sensitivity: Task 2's $0.10/MMBtu/day led to -$40M on a contract that should have been profitable. In commodity trading, cost assumptions can dwarf the underlying trade P&L. Always sanity-check parameters against market rates.

"What's dynamic programming?"

Breaking an optimization problem into overlapping subproblems and caching solutions to avoid recomputation. In the FICO bucketing problem, finding optimal boundaries for k buckets over N scores brute-force costs O(N^k) — infeasible for k=10. DP reduces it to O(N² × k) by observing that the optimal k-bucket solution over 0 to i always contains an optimal (k-1)-bucket solution over some prefix 0 to m.

"What is expected loss and why does it matter?"

EL = PD × LGD × EAD — the regulatory formula (Basel III) for the average loss a bank expects on a loan. Banks must hold capital reserves proportional to their EL. A PD model lets you quantify this precisely rather than applying a blanket provision rate. Better PD models = more accurate capital allocation = competitive advantage in loan pricing.
